What Does It Cost to Run a Million Tokens on Each Major Model?

GPT-5 lists at $1.25/$10 per million tokens and GPT-5.5 at $5/$30. DeepSeek V3.2 charges $0.28/$0.42. The table is the easy part — caching, batching, context tiers and tokenizers decide the real bill.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The invoice arrived on a Tuesday in late October 2026, and the finance lead at a mid-size software company in Austin stared at the line she could not explain. Her team had moved from one flagship model to another — a cheaper one, on paper — and the monthly bill had gone up 40 percent. The per-token price had fallen. The invoice had not. She called the engineering lead, who called the vendor, and nobody in the chain could say, in one sentence, what a million tokens actually cost.

That is the normal state of affairs in inference pricing. Every major lab publishes a price per million tokens, and no two of them mean quite the same thing by it. The table below is the honest starting point: standard real-time rates, shortest context band, from each lab’s own list price as checked on November 17, 2026 by InferenceHub’s price-change tracker and confirmed against BenchLeader’s pricing tracker on November 19. Treat every number as a snapshot with a date on it, because in this market a price chart without a date is a historical document.

The first thing the table teaches is that output tokens are the bill. Input is the cheap half of every row: output runs four to eight times the input rate, and in a reasoning-heavy workload output is most of what you buy. Take a plain job — one million input tokens, half a million output. On GPT-5 that is $1.25 plus $5.00, or $6.25. On Claude Opus 5.5 it is $4.00 plus $10.00, or $14. On DeepSeek V3.2 it is $0.28 plus $0.21, or $0.49. Same shape of work, a 29-fold spread between the cheapest and the dearest, before a single discount is applied. Any comparison that quotes only the input column is quoting the smaller number on purpose.

The second thing the table hides is context. Google’s Gemini 3 Pro charges $2 per million input tokens up to 200,000 tokens, then $4 above that threshold, with output rising from $12 to $18 in the same step. Anthropic charges no such surcharge: every current Claude model except Haiku 5.5 includes its full one-million-token window at standard rates. Run the arithmetic on a 500,000-token document with a 50,000-token answer. On Gemini 3 Pro, past the 200K line, that is $2.00 of input and $0.90 of output — $2.90. On Claude Sonnet 5.5, with no tier change, it is $1.00 plus $0.50 — $1.50. The model with the same $2 headline input rate costs nearly twice as much on the long document, and the difference is a footnote on the pricing page, not a line on the invoice.

Then there is caching, the largest discount in inference pricing and the one most teams leave on the table. A cached prompt prefix — the system instructions, the retrieved documents, the conversation so far — is billed at a fraction of the fresh rate. Anthropic bills cache reads at 10 percent of the standard input rate on most models, 5 percent on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, and 2.5 percent on its Fable 5.1 flagship, against a 1.25-times charge to write the cache. OpenAI bills cached input at roughly 10 percent on its flagship tiers and 5 percent on GPT-6.1 Sol. DeepSeek charges $0.028 per million cached tokens against $0.28 fresh — a 90 percent cut. The math turns fast. An agent loop that re-sends a 100,000-token system prompt a thousand times burns 100 million fresh input tokens: $200 on Sonnet 5.5 at the standard rate. Served from cache at $0.10 per million, the same reads cost $10, plus about $0.25 to write the cache once. Caching does not shave the bill. It divides it by twenty.

Batching does the same thing on a different axis. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google all price batch or asynchronous requests at 50 percent of standard rates — input and output alike — in exchange for results within 24 hours. OpenAI’s flex processing extends batch rates to ordinary synchronous calls that may run slower or be refused when capacity is short. Google’s Gemini API offers a flex tier at the same 50 percent discount with a one-to-fifteen-minute target. DeepSeek documents no batch API at all, and instead halves its rates outside weekday peak hours. The decision is about the workload, not the vendor: anything that can wait a day — nightly classification, report generation, bulk summarization — costs half as much as the identical tokens bought in real time. A team running 10 million input tokens a night through GPT-5 pays $12.50 in real time and $6.25 in batch. The tokens are the same. The clock is the discount.

The subtlest charge is the one no price table shows: the tokenizer. A token is not a fixed amount of text, and models bill different token counts for the same characters. TokenWatt’s November 5, 2026 measurement sent the same 263,763 characters of code, documentation and agent tool output through six model families. Claude Opus 5.5 billed 108,871 tokens for it. GPT-5.5 billed 68,147 — 37 percent fewer. The consequence is concrete: Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Gemini 3 Pro both list at $2 per million input tokens, yet on the same code Sonnet costs $0.83 per million characters against Gemini’s $0.60. Same list price, 37 percent apart, because one tokenizer is thirstier than the other. Any buyer comparing input prices across labs without adjusting for tokenization is comparing prices in two currencies.

And the table itself expires. Gemini 4 Carbon launched at $2/$10 and doubles to $4/$20 on December 1, 2026, according to InferenceHub’s November 17 check. GPT-5.5 costs four times GPT-5’s input price for the same vendor’s flagship badge. Anthropic launched Sonnet 5 at $2/$10 and cancelled a planned rise back to $3/$15 that had been scheduled for September 1, 2026. Prices in this market move in weeks, sometimes in days, and the most-shared price charts go stale within days of publication. A procurement decision built on a chart from August is a decision built on prices that no longer exist.

The price list is the beginning of the bill, not the bill.

The honest objection

A price table invites a false precision, and this one is no exception. First, list prices are not contract prices: enterprise buyers negotiate volume discounts, committed-use tiers and provisioned throughput that can move the numbers by half, and none of that appears here. Second, reasoning tokens are billed as output, so a model that “thinks” for 20,000 tokens before answering has already spent more than the table suggests — the thinking is the invoice. Third, the cheapest row is not the cheapest answer. DeepSeek V3.2’s $0.49 workload and Opus 5.5’s $14 workload are not the same product: a model that needs three attempts to finish a task costs more than a dearer model that finishes it once, and the table says nothing about attempts. The right reading of the table is narrower than it looks. It prices tokens. It does not price finished work.

What to do with the numbers

Price by finished task, at stated effort settings, with stated cache assumptions, and a date on every number. Measure your own input-to-output ratio before choosing a model — a chat product and a document pipeline buy different token mixes, and the model that wins one loses the other. Cache the stable prefix of every repeated prompt; the first reuse pays for the write, and every reuse after that is nearly free. Batch everything that can wait a day, and check whether your off-peak traffic qualifies for DeepSeek’s halved rates. Re-run the comparison monthly, because the board changes faster than any procurement cycle. And keep an open-weights option in the evaluation set: DeepSeek V3.2 at $0.28/$0.42 is the fallback that disciplines every frontier price above it.

The buyers who win this market will not be the ones who found the cheapest row. They will be the ones who stopped buying tokens and started buying finished tasks — measured this week, at their own ratios, on a table they dated themselves.
