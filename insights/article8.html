How to Benchmark an LLM for Your Own Workload

The public leaderboard says a model scores 90% on MMLU. Your traffic says it answers 61% of your customers correctly. The leaderboard is not wrong — it is measuring something else. How to build an evaluation that measures your work.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The team had done everything the leaderboard told them to do. They picked the model at the top of the public rankings, the one with the highest MMLU score and the best reasoning results, and they put it into production. Two weeks later the support desk was full of the same complaint: the model was answering questions confidently and incorrectly, it was slow on the requests that mattered most, and it was costing twice what the budget assumed. The leaderboard was not lying. It was measuring something else. MMLU tests whether a model can answer multiple-choice questions about high school physics. The team's customers were asking about warranty terms, and the model had never been tested on warranty terms. The gap between a public benchmark and a production workload is not a rounding error. It is the whole evaluation.

The reason public benchmarks mislead is not that they are badly designed. It is that they are designed for a different purpose. A public benchmark exists to compare models against each other on a fixed task, under fixed conditions, so that the comparison is reproducible. A production workload exists to serve a specific set of users asking specific questions under specific constraints — latency, cost, accuracy on the long tail of real requests. The two purposes overlap less than the leaderboard rankings suggest, and the overlap shrinks further every year, because the benchmarks themselves are contaminated by the training data that produced the models they are testing. The fix is not a better leaderboard. It is a benchmark built from your own traffic, run on your own terms, and kept private.

Why the leaderboard number is not your number

The most cited evidence for the gap is a single model measured three different ways. LLaMA-65B scored 0.637 on MMLU under Stanford's HELM harness, 0.488 under Hugging Face's lm-eval-harness, and 0.636 under the original MMLU implementation. Same model, same benchmark, same questions — a 15-point spread, caused by differences in prompting, scoring, and formatting that the leaderboard does not disclose. The model did not change. The measurement did. If a single model can produce three different MMLU scores, then a ranking that compares two models on one implementation is measuring the implementation as much as the model, and a buyer who picks a vendor on the strength of that ranking is picking a harness.

Contamination makes the gap worse. GSM8K, the 8,500-problem grade-school math benchmark, is contaminated — the problems and their solutions appear in training data, and the benchmark's own authors responded by building GSM1k, a set of new problems with the same structure and different numbers. LiveBench refreshes its questions monthly and still found top models scoring below 70 percent at publication, because fresh questions are the only ones that measure reasoning rather than recall. The decontamination tools in lm-eval-harness exist, but they are off by default, and most leaderboard runs do not enable them. A public score is therefore a number that may measure memorization, may measure reasoning, and may measure the difference between two harness implementations — and the leaderboard does not say which.

Build the dataset from your own traffic

The first step is the dataset, and the dataset should come from your own traffic. Take a sample of real requests — a few hundred is enough to start, a thousand is better — and have a human score the model's answers against a rubric you write yourself. The rubric is the whole evaluation: it defines what a good answer looks like in your context, which is the thing no public benchmark can define for you. A support team cares about whether the answer resolves the ticket. A legal team cares about whether the answer cites the right clause. A coding team cares about whether the code compiles. None of those are measured by MMLU, and none of them are measured by HELM's 16 core scenarios across 7 metrics, which is a fine instrument for comparing models in general and a useless one for comparing them for your work.

The dataset has to be hidden. The moment the evaluation set is public, it is training data, and the next model trained on it will score higher on it without being better at the work. LiveCodeBench solves this by continuously collecting new problems, tagged by release date, so that a model's score on problems released after its training cutoff measures real capability. LiveBench solves it by refreshing monthly. Your evaluation solves it by never publishing the set. Keep it in a private repository, version it, and add new cases every month from the requests the model got wrong. The evaluation that does not change is the evaluation that stops measuring anything.

Measure cost and latency with the accuracy

The second step is to measure cost and latency alongside accuracy, because the model that wins on accuracy and loses on cost is not the model you can deploy. The public data on speed is useful as a starting point and dangerous as a decision: BenchLeader's public data, current as of November 19, 2026, tracks 212 models with a median output speed near 80 tokens per second and a median time to first token of 1.1 seconds, but those are medians across providers, and the spread between the fastest and slowest provider for the same model is often twofold or more. Artificial Analysis publishes provider-by-provider measurements updated through October and November 2026, and the numbers change month to month as providers re-route, re-optimize, and re-price. The figures in this article are snapshots from November 17 to 19, 2026, and they will be stale by the time you read them. That is the point: speed and price are not properties of the model. They are properties of the provider, the routing, and the month — and they have to be measured on your own traffic, on the provider you will actually use.

The cost measurement is the one teams skip, and it is the one that matters most. A model that scores 3 percent higher on your dataset but costs twice as much per token is not a better model. It is a more expensive one, and the 3 percent has to be worth the doubling. Measure the cost per completed task — not per token, per task — because the token count depends on the prompt, the prompt depends on the model, and the model that needs a longer prompt to get the right answer is not the cheaper model even if its per-token price is lower. The fastest model is not always the cheapest, and the cheapest model is not always the fastest, and the model that wins on your dataset may lose on both. The evaluation that reports accuracy without cost and latency is not an evaluation. It is a press release.

The method, in short

The method is four steps, and none of them is optional. Sample real requests from your own traffic. Score them against a rubric you wrote, with a human in the loop. Measure accuracy, cost per completed task, and latency together, on the provider you will actually use. And re-run the evaluation every month, with new cases from the requests the model got wrong, because the model that is best this month may not be best next month, and the workload that is stable this month may not be stable next month. The public leaderboard is a starting point for the shortlist, not a decision. The decision is your data, your rubric, your traffic, and your budget — and the benchmark that measures those things is the only one that matters.

There is a counterargument, and it is worth hearing before you build the whole apparatus. A private evaluation is expensive to build and maintain, and for many teams the public leaderboard is good enough — because their workload is close enough to the public tasks that the ranking transfers, or because the differences between the top models are smaller than the cost of measuring them. That is a fair argument, and it is true for some teams. But it is not true for the teams that discover, two weeks after launch, that the model at the top of the leaderboard answers their customers wrong, slowly, and expensively. The cost of building the evaluation is measured in days. The cost of skipping it is measured in quarters.
